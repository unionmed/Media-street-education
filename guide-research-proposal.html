RESEARCH PREPARATION

Turn a broad interest into a research proposal

A proposal is a reasoned case for a project you can carry out. Use a short concept note to expose the questions you still need to answer before writing the full application.

A Merit Way editorial guide · 21 September 2026

In this guide
  1. Separate the topic from the question
  2. Read to position the problem
  3. Match the method to the evidence needed
  4. Adapt to the department

Separate the topic from the question

“AI in education” is a topic. A question identifies what you want to understand, for whom and in what setting. Draft three possible questions, then select the one you can investigate with plausible evidence and access. Explain why the question matters in a short paragraph. Avoid promising to transform an entire sector when the proposed work concerns one bounded setting.

Read to position the problem

Create a reading table with four columns: source, question, method and what it leaves unresolved. Use it to compare studies instead of collecting quotations. Your first reading should help narrow the question and show how the proposed work connects to existing knowledge. Keep accurate references as you go. If you use AI to organise notes, verify every reference against the original publication.

Match the method to the evidence needed

Explain what material could answer your question and how you would obtain and analyse it. Interviews, documents and numerical data serve different purposes. Consider the practical constraints: recruitment, permissions, language, time and your own methodological preparation. Write down the most likely obstacle and a realistic alternative. This makes the proposal more credible than naming an impressive method without an access plan.

Adapt to the department

Edinburgh’s application guidance points applicants to a proposal structure and practical writing advice. Use university guidance as a starting point, then follow the exact requirements of the department or funding call you are applying to. Check length, headings, references and whether contact with a supervisor is expected. Finish by asking a reader to explain your question back to you; if they cannot, simplify it.
